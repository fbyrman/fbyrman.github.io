3 How to quantify hyperbolicity

So how do you measure how hyperbolic a model is? The curvature alone does not tell you much. What matters is how curved the space is where the embeddings actually end up. And since the curvature only enters through the exponential map, the most direct test is to compare what the model computes just before that map with what it computes just after it.

Hyperbolic VLMs build their losses out of three geometric operations: the distance between two embeddings, and the exterior angle and half-aperture of the entailment cones that encode the hierarchy. Figure 6 shows each one before and after the map; the ratio between the two tells you how much the map changed it.

Figure 6. Distance (top), exterior angle (middle) and half-aperture (bottom), before (left) and after (right) the exponential map.

Comparing each operation before and after the map gives three distortion ratios, and we derive a closed-form expression for each. They turn out to depend on just four numbers: the norms $r_1$ and $r_2$ of the encoder outputs, the angle $\theta$ between them, and the curvature $c$. The distance ratio, for example, is the hyperbolic law of cosines divided by the Euclidean one:

\[ r_d(r_1, r_2, \theta; c) = \frac{\operatorname{arcosh}\!\big( \cosh(\sqrt{c}\, r_1) \cosh(\sqrt{c}\, r_2) - \sinh(\sqrt{c}\, r_1) \sinh(\sqrt{c}\, r_2) \cos\theta \big)} {\sqrt{c}\, \sqrt{r_1^2 + r_2^2 - 2 r_1 r_2 \cos\theta}}. \]

At a given curvature, the distance ratio is therefore a function of three numbers, and fills a cube over $(r_1, r_2, \theta)$; the same goes for the exterior angle ratio. To read the cube, we fix $r_1$ and take a two-dimensional slice over $r_2$ and $\theta$, as in Figure 7. In the next section, $r_1$ will be the most common radius of a model's text embeddings.
