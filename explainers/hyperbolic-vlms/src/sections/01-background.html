1 Background

1.1 Learning from images and captions

One of the most influential papers in recent AI is CLIP (Radford et al., 2021). Its insight: the internet is full of images that come with a caption, and that pairing can be used as a powerful training signal. An image encoder and a text encoder map their inputs into one shared latent space, and the model is rewarded for placing each image closer to its own caption than to all the mismatched ones.

Figure 1. Contrastive pre-training on a batch of three pairs. Each image vector $I_i$ is compared with each text vector $T_j$. The highlighted cells on the diagonal are the true pairs, whose similarity should go up; every other cell is a mismatch, whose similarity should go down. Hover over a cell to see which image and caption it compares.

Training is a matching game. For a batch of $N$ pairs, every image is asked which of the $N$ captions is its own, and every caption which image. The contrastive loss is low only when each one picks its true partner. It never says where things should go, only how they should relate. Try it in Figure 2: press Train and watch the matching pairs find each other on the sphere while the different pairs spread as far apart as they can.

Figure 2. A three-dimensional latent space. Squares are image embeddings, rings are caption embeddings, and each pair shares a colour. The matrix on the right shows the cosine similarity of every image with every caption; training drives the diagonal to 1. The loss uses a temperature of $\tau = 0.3$.

Real models do the same with hundreds of dimensions and batches of tens of thousands of pairs, and what comes out is a space organized by meaning. That makes zero-shot classification possible: write a caption per class, “a photo of a dog”, and pick the one closest to the image. Retrieval works the same way in either direction: embed a text query and return the closest images, or embed an image and return the closest captions.

1.2 Hierarchies and hyperbolic space

A recurring lesson in machine learning is that models built to match the structure of their data learn more from less. The space in which a model embeds its data is one such design choice, and CLIP chose the unit sphere. On a sphere every embedding has the same length, so all meaning has to live in its direction. That is enough to tell whether a caption belongs to an image, but not whether one concept is more general than another. And vision-language data arguably has that ordering: animal is more general than dog, and a caption more general than the image it describes.

Figure 3. Vision-language data read as a hierarchy, from general words down to specific images. Hover over a node to see everything it falls under.

If the data is organized this way, the obvious first step is to drop the normalization and use all of Euclidean space, letting the distance from the origin express how general something is. But trees fit poorly there. The number of nodes multiplies with every level, while the room at a given distance from the origin only grows in proportion to that distance, so deep levels get squeezed together. In hyperbolic space that room grows exponentially, as fast as a tree branches. Figure 4 shows a tree in the Poincaré disk, a map of the entire hyperbolic plane onto a disc. All edges have the same length; they only look shorter near the rim, where the map squeezes in ever more space.

Figure 4. A binary tree in the Poincaré disk, with every edge of hyperbolic length 1. Click a node to move it to the centre. The move preserves all lengths, and the tree looks the same from every node.

This is the idea behind hyperbolic vision-language models such as MERU (Desai et al., 2023), HyCoCLIP (Pal et al., 2025), ARGENT (Huynh et al., 2026) and PHyCLIP (Yoshikawa and Matsubara, 2026). They keep CLIP's recipe but embed images and text in hyperbolic space, with general concepts near the origin and specific ones further out. The encoders themselves are still ordinary Euclidean networks, though. Their outputs only reach hyperbolic space at the very end, through a single exponential map, shown in Figure 5.

Figure 5. The exponential map, the one hyperbolic step in the model. An encoder output $\mathbf{v}$ (blue) lies in the flat plane that touches the hyperboloid at its origin. The map walks the same distance in the same direction along the surface, landing at $\mathbf{x}$ (orange). Shorten $\mathbf{v}$ and see how little the map then changes: near the origin the surface is almost flat.
