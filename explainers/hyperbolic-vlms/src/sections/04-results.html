4 How hyperbolic are they?

4.1 Distance distortion

We trained MERU, HyCoCLIP, ARGENT and PHyCLIP on CC3M, about three million image-caption pairs. All four start at curvature $c = 1$ and push it down to its lower bound of $c = 0.1$ within the first tenth of training, while their embeddings settle at radii between roughly 0.2 and 1.1. Figure 8 cuts the cube of Figure 7 at each model's most common text radius and final curvature. In every panel, the exponential map changes distances by less than 1%.

Figure 8. The distance ratio between a text embedding at the model's most common text radius $r_1$ and an image embedding at radius $r_2$, at the converged curvature $c = 0.1$ (ViT-S/16; for PHyCLIP the mean over its 64 factors). All four panels share one colour scale.

4.2 Removing the exponential map at inference

If the exponential map is close to the identity, removing it at read-out should go unnoticed. To test this, we evaluate every trained model twice: once as usual, and once with the exponential map deleted, so that images and captions are compared by their Euclidean distance before the map. Table 1 shows that classification and retrieval scores barely move, and do not even move in a consistent direction. The publicly released MERU and HyCoCLIP checkpoints, trained on far more data, behave the same way.

4.3 Training without the exponential map

That the map can be removed after training does not mean the curvature played no role during training. To test this, we train a Euclidean control for every model from scratch: the same losses, data and schedule, but with every operation computed in Euclidean space and the exponential map replaced by the identity. The two runs only differ in the first 10,000 or so steps, while the curvature is still on its way down to its lower bound. From then on, their radii, losses and temperatures are nearly identical, and so are their final scores (Table 2). The Euclidean controls come out slightly ahead more often than not, but we do not read this as Euclidean models being better: the point is how close the two are.
