1 Background

1.1 Predicting through concepts

Machine learning is increasingly deployed in high-stakes domains such as medicine and finance. There, the reasoning behind a prediction often matters more than the prediction itself, which motivates explainable AI. Concept bottleneck models (CBMs) (Koh et al., 2020) make that reasoning explicit by predicting in two steps:

  1. a network maps the input to scores for a set of human-interpretable concepts;
  2. a linear layer predicts the label from those scores alone.

The concept layer is the bottleneck: nothing reaches the output without passing through it. So every prediction comes with its reasons, and an expert who disagrees with one can correct it and watch the prediction change. Try it in Figure 1.

Figure 1. A toy CBM. The concept predictor scores the image on six concepts, and a linear layer turns the scores into class probabilities. Drag stripes up and saddle down, and the horse becomes a zebra.

1.2 Where the concepts come from

Training the concept predictor requires a concept set and, in the original formulation, concept labels for every training image. Early CBMs therefore relied on expert annotation: bird attributes such as wing colour and bill shape in CUB-200-2011 (Wah et al., 2011), or radiologist-graded features such as joint space narrowing in knee X-rays from the Osteoarthritis Initiative (Nevitt et al., 2006). Such annotations are costly and exist for few datasets.

Recent work removes this requirement with two large pretrained models. A large language model generates the concept set, typically by listing the visual features of each class (Oikarinen et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023). A vision-language model such as CLIP (Radford et al., 2021), which embeds images and text in a shared space, then scores each image on each concept by the similarity of their embeddings. This makes CBMs applicable to any dataset, but the concept set is still fixed in advance, independent of what the vision model has actually learned.
