4.1 One forward pass against 150 epochs

On CNNs, a single forward pass of ScaleGMN-B beats 150 epochs of SGD on the objective it was trained for. Without the penalty it lifts the average accuracy of the unseen test networks from 37.9% to 50.3%, where SGD reaches 44.5%. With the penalty it reaches a lower test loss than 150 epochs of SGD (1.901 against 2.070) and cuts the L1 norm of the weights by 88%, against 71% for SGD, though at a lower accuracy (37.4% against 41.6%). It does all this in about 0.05 seconds per network, where SGD takes six to eight minutes.

On MLPs the picture is mixed. With the penalty, the metanetwork matches the test loss of 150 epochs of SGD (1.964 against 1.963) with a far sparser network, 85% against 55%, but at a lower accuracy. Without the penalty it does about as well as 25 epochs of SGD. We attribute this to the smaller MLP zoo, which gives the metanetwork less to generalize from.

SGD would keep improving with more epochs, so it is not a competitor to be beaten forever. It is a reference point: a metanetwork that has never seen these networks, or these test images, gets in one step where an optimizer tuned to each network needs minutes.

4.2 Does the equivariance help?

For MLPs, yes. Without canonicalization, the metanetwork trains less efficiently, and the symmetry-broken GMN-B ends behind ScaleGMN-B on every accuracy, loss and sparsity measure in Table 1.

For CNNs the difference is small. The training curves of the two models are largely similar, and on plain cross-entropy the symmetry-broken model is even slightly ahead. The regularized symmetry-broken run did not finish, because of numerical instabilities, which makes its final numbers less conclusive.

This is exactly what Section 2.3 predicts. A CNN has one scaling symmetry per channel, an MLP one per neuron, so building in scale equivariance has much less to offer for a CNN. Kalogeropoulos et al. saw the same pattern: their largest gains from scale equivariance were on MLPs, and their gains on CNNs were modest.
