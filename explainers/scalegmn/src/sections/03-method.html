3 Method

Our metanetwork is a Scale Equivariant Graph Metanetwork (ScaleGMN, Kalogeropoulos et al., 2024). It reads a trained network and returns new weights for it, and it has the symmetries of Section 2 built in.

3.1 Networks as graphs

The metanetwork reads the input network as a graph: neurons are vertices that carry their biases, and connections are edges that carry their weights (Kofinas et al., 2024). For a CNN, channels are the vertices and kernels sit on the edges. A graph has no built-in order, so reordering the neurons of a layer changes nothing for the metanetwork. Permutation symmetry comes for free.

3.2 Equivariance

For scaling, every step inside the metanetwork first brings its inputs to a canonical form, in which all rescaled copies of a neuron look the same. The result is a metanetwork that is equivariant: permute or rescale the input network, and the output network is permuted or rescaled in exactly the same way. In Figure 6 that means both routes around the square end at the same network. An ordinary metanetwork has no such guarantee. It has to learn the symmetries from data, and only ever approximately.

Figure 6. Equivariance. Going right applies the metanetwork, going down applies the symmetry. Bottom right is reached by applying the symmetry first; edges where it differs from the other route are outlined. Both metanetworks are simple stand-in maps, not trained models: the equivariant one updates each neuron's weights from that neuron alone, the ordinary one adds a fixed correction to every storage position.

3.3 Training

We train on a model zoo: a large collection of small networks, all trained to classify grayscale CIFAR-10 images (Krizhevsky, 2009). For CNNs we use the public Small CNN Zoo (Unterthiner et al., 2021); for MLPs we built our own. For each network in a batch, the metanetwork proposes new weights, and we measure how well the proposed network classifies. Optionally we add a penalty on the size of the weights, which pushes many of them to zero and makes the network sparse. The error flows back through the proposed network into the metanetwork: we train the metanetwork, never the networks in the zoo.

At test time the metanetwork fine-tunes networks, and images, it has never seen, in a single forward pass. As a reference we fine-tune the same test networks with SGD for 25 to 150 epochs. To isolate what scale equivariance contributes, we also train the same metanetwork without its canonicalization step. In the results, our metanetwork is called ScaleGMN-B and this symmetry-broken version GMN-B.
