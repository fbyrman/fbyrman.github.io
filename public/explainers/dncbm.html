Revisiting Discover-then-Name Concept Bottleneck Models: A Reproducibility Study

F. Byrman, E. Kasteleyn, B. Kuipers, D. Uyterlinde

Transactions on Machine Learning Research, 2025

1 Background

1.1 Predicting through concepts

Machine learning is increasingly deployed in high-stakes domains such as medicine and finance. There, the reasoning behind a prediction often matters more than the prediction itself, which motivates explainable AI. Concept bottleneck models (CBMs) (Koh et al., 2020) make that reasoning explicit by predicting in two steps:

  1. a network maps the input to scores for a set of human-interpretable concepts;
  2. a linear layer predicts the label from those scores alone.

The concept layer is the bottleneck: nothing reaches the output without passing through it. So every prediction comes with its reasons, and an expert who disagrees with one can correct it and watch the prediction change. Try it in Figure 1.

Figure 1. A toy CBM. The concept predictor scores the image on six concepts, and a linear layer turns the scores into class probabilities. Drag stripes up and saddle down, and the horse becomes a zebra.

1.2 Where the concepts come from

Training the concept predictor requires a concept set and, in the original formulation, concept labels for every training image. Early CBMs therefore relied on expert annotation: bird attributes such as wing colour and bill shape in CUB-200-2011 (Wah et al., 2011), or radiologist-graded features such as joint space narrowing in knee X-rays from the Osteoarthritis Initiative (Nevitt et al., 2006). Such annotations are costly and exist for few datasets.

Recent work removes this requirement with two large pretrained models. A large language model generates the concept set, typically by listing the visual features of each class (Oikarinen et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023). A vision-language model such as CLIP (Radford et al., 2021), which embeds images and text in a shared space, then scores each image on each concept by the similarity of their embeddings. This makes CBMs applicable to any dataset, but the concept set is still fixed in advance, independent of what the vision model has actually learned.

2 Discover-then-Name CBMs

Discover-then-Name CBMs (DN-CBMs) (Rao et al., 2024) turn this around. Rather than fixing a concept set in advance and looking for it in images, they find the concepts a vision model already knows, then names them (Figure 2):

  1. Discover. A sparse autoencoder (Bricken et al., 2023) learns to reconstruct CLIP image embeddings through 8,192 mostly silent neurons. Its decoder is then dropped.
  2. Name. Each neuron gets the word, out of 20,000, whose CLIP text embedding is closest to its direction.
  3. Classify. A sparse linear probe maps the neurons to classes.

Figure 2. Top: the autoencoder is trained on reconstruction alone, without labels. Bottom: the trained encoder explaining a Places365 image; bars show each concept's contribution. Hover over a concept to find its neuron.

3 How a neuron gets its name

Each neuron has a direction in CLIP space, its dictionary vector $\mathbf{p}_c$: its row of the dropped decoder. It is named after the most similar word:

\[ s_c = \argmax_{w} \; \cos\big(\mathbf{p}_c,\, T(w)\big), \]

and the fit of that name is its cosine score $v_c = \cos(\mathbf{p}_c, T(s_c))$. Our main finding is that nothing in training pushes $\mathbf{p}_c$ towards any word. The autoencoder only reconstructs; names come afterwards. So the fit is left to chance, and a neuron far from every word still gets a name (Figure 3).

Figure 3. Naming in a two-dimensional sketch of CLIP space. Drag a neuron (coloured) and its name jumps to the nearest word (grey). In CLIP's 1,024 dimensions words lie much further apart: typical scores are around 0.15.

4 When the names don't fit

The original paper shows its best-aligned neurons, and they convince. But cosine scores across the network fall from 0.42 to about 0, and the worst-aligned neurons fire for images that have nothing to do with their names (Figure 4).

Figure 4. The top-activating images per dataset for four neurons, best- or worst-aligned.

These neurons drive predictions. DN-CBM explains a horse with pelican, michigan and aaliyah (Figure 5): a correct prediction with an unfaithful explanation.

Figure 5. The concepts that contribute most to one prediction of the original DN-CBM.

5 Pulling neurons towards their names

We give training the incentive it lacks. For 30 more epochs we fine-tune the autoencoder with an extra term that rewards active neurons $\mathbf{z}$ for pointing at their names (Figure 6):

\[ \mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\|\mathrm{SAE}(\mathbf{a}) - \mathbf{a}\|_2^2 + \lambda_1 \|\mathbf{z}\|_1}_{\text{original objective}} \; - \; C \, \frac{\mathbf{z}}{\|\mathbf{z}\|_2} \cdot \mathbf{v}. \]

Weighting by activation pulls hardest on the neurons that drive predictions. Too large a $C$ spreads activations thinly over many neurons; at $C = 10^{-4}$ the mean cosine score rises from 0.15 to 0.54.

Figure 6. Fine-tuning rotates each neuron towards its name, raising its cosine score. Press fine-tune.

The worst-aligned neurons now fire for what they are called (Figure 7), and explanations become meaningful (Figure 8). In a user study with 203 participants, fine-tuned concepts were rated a better match for their images at every alignment level, significantly so for the middle and worst groups, at $p < 0.001$.

Figure 7. The worst-aligned neurons of Figure 4, before and after fine-tuning.

Figure 8. Figure 5 again, now next to our fine-tuned model (blue). Each model has its own scale: after fine-tuning, activations are spread over more neurons.

The cost is 0.7 to 4.1 points of accuracy (Table 1). Aligned names also sometimes just repeat the class: horses for a horse is faithful but says little.

6 Conclusion

DN-CBMs name their neurons after training, and nothing in training ties a neuron to its name. Many names do not fit, so the explanations built on them are not faithful. A short fine-tune that rewards alignment makes the names fit, at a small cost in accuracy.
