A Principled Bias Initialization for the Sigmoid Loss

F. Byrman

1 Contrastive learning

Vision-language models such as CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) learn from images and their web captions. Two encoders map images and text into one shared space, and training pulls each image towards its own caption and away from the others in the batch. Everything this contrastive learning needs is one matrix: the similarity of every image to every caption.

Figure 1. A batch of three pairs. Matches lie on the diagonal. Hover over a cell to see what it compares.

CLIP trains on this matrix with a softmax loss: each row is a multiple-choice question, which caption belongs to this image?, and each column the same question for a caption. With similarity $\kappa$ and temperature $\tau$:

\[ \mathcal{L}_\text{softmax} = -\frac{1}{2|\mathcal{B}|} \sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{B}|} \left[ \log \frac{e^{\kappa(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{y}_i)/\tau}}{\sum_{j} e^{\kappa(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{y}_j)/\tau}} + \log \frac{e^{\kappa(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{y}_i)/\tau}}{\sum_{j} e^{\kappa(\mathbf{x}_j, \mathbf{y}_i)/\tau}} \right]. \]

The sigmoid loss of SigLIP (Zhai et al., 2023) instead asks a yes-or-no question for every cell, is this a match?, with label $z_{ij} = +1$ on the diagonal and $-1$ elsewhere, temperature $t$ and bias $b$:

\[ \mathcal{L}_\text{sigmoid} = -\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|} \sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{B}|} \sum_{j=1}^{|\mathcal{B}|} \log \sigma\big( z_{ij} \, ( t\, \kappa(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{y}_j) + b ) \big), \qquad \sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}. \]

Softmax is relative: a caption only has to beat the others in its row. Sigmoid is absolute: every pair has to clear a threshold on its own (Figure 2).

Figure 2. One batch, scored by softmax (left) and sigmoid (right). Each cell is the predicted chance that photo and caption match; gold frames mark the true pairs. Moving one similarity shifts a whole row under softmax, but a single cell under sigmoid.

2 The role of the bias term

Take cosine similarity, and a model with randomly initialized weights, so that the average similarity between an image and a caption is 0. Without a bias, every pair then gets a predicted chance of $\sigma(0) = \tfrac{1}{2}$ of being a match. That is far too optimistic: of the $|\mathcal{B}|^2$ pairs in a batch, only $|\mathcal{B}|$ match, so the true rate, the batch prior, is $1/|\mathcal{B}|$ (Figure 3).

Figure 3. Every image paired with every caption in a batch. Only the $|\mathcal{B}|$ pairs on the diagonal are matches, so the share of matches, $1/|\mathcal{B}|$, shrinks as the batch grows.

The bias fixes this. It shifts every logit $t\,\kappa + b$, so a negative bias makes the model sceptical by default. SigLIP initializes it at $b = -10$, which consistently improves performance across all tasks: training starts close to the prior, and the first steps no longer over-correct (Zhai et al., 2023).

That number hides two assumptions: a fixed batch size, since the prior changes with $|\mathcal{B}|$, and a similarity centred at zero, since with mean similarity $\mu$ the model starts at $\sigma(t\,\mu + b)$. Figure 4 shows what happens when either one fails.

Figure 4. Where training starts: the predicted chance of a match at the mean similarity $\mu$ and temperature $t$. It should start on the batch prior (green). Without a bias (grey) it starts near ½; SigLIP's $b = -10$ (orange) only lands on it for $\mu = 0$ and a batch of about 22,000, whatever the temperature. Prior matching (blue) always does.

3 Prior-matching initialization

The fix is to choose $b$ so that the model starts exactly on the prior, $\sigma(t\,\mu + b) = 1/|\mathcal{B}|$. Solving for $b$:

\[ \boxed{\; b^\star = -\ln\big(|\mathcal{B}| - 1\big) - t\,\mu \;} \]

The first term handles the batch size, the second the mean similarity. For cosine similarity and a batch of 512, this gives $b^\star \approx -6.2$ rather than $-10$. The only extra cost is estimating $\mu$: the mean similarity over a few batches, before training starts.

4 Preliminary results

This was a side contribution of my thesis, and a full study is not worth the compute to me, but I wanted to show something. So I trained two ViT-S/16 models, one per loss, for 50,000 steps at batch size 512 on ImageNet with synthetic captions. They are hyperbolic, with the negated Lorentzian distance as similarity, and as far as I know this is the first use of the sigmoid loss in hyperbolic space. With $b = -10$ training never settles; with prior matching ($\mu$ from four batches) it trains stably.

The sigmoid loss wins zero-shot classification on six of seven benchmarks, by 15 points on CIFAR-10 (Table 1), but trails softmax on retrieval (Table 2). One run per loss, no tuning: a first look, not a verdict.

5 Conclusion

The bias decides where sigmoid-loss training starts. SigLIP's $b = -10$ assumes a fixed batch size and a similarity centred at zero; prior matching drops both assumptions at the cost of estimating one number. That was enough to train with the sigmoid loss in hyperbolic space.
