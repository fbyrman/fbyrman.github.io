How Hyperbolic Are Hyperbolic Vision-Language Models?

F. Byrman, P. Das, S. Verdenius, P. Mettes

NeurIPS 2026 NeurReps Workshop, Proceedings Track

1 Background

1.1 Learning from images and captions

One of the most influential papers in recent AI is CLIP (Radford et al., 2021). Its insight: the internet is full of images that come with a caption, and that pairing can be used as a powerful training signal. An image encoder and a text encoder map their inputs into one shared latent space, and the model is rewarded for placing each image closer to its own caption than to all the mismatched ones.

Figure 1. Contrastive pre-training on a batch of three pairs. Each image vector $I_i$ is compared with each text vector $T_j$. The highlighted cells on the diagonal are the true pairs, whose similarity should go up; every other cell is a mismatch, whose similarity should go down. Hover over a cell to see which image and caption it compares.

Training is a matching game. For a batch of $N$ pairs, every image is asked which of the $N$ captions is its own, and every caption which image. The contrastive loss is low only when each one picks its true partner. It never says where things should go, only how they should relate. Try it in Figure 2: press Train and watch the matching pairs find each other on the sphere while the different pairs spread as far apart as they can.

Figure 2. A three-dimensional latent space. Squares are image embeddings, rings are caption embeddings, and each pair shares a colour. The matrix on the right shows the cosine similarity of every image with every caption; training drives the diagonal to 1. The loss uses a temperature of $\tau = 0.3$.

Real models do the same with hundreds of dimensions and batches of tens of thousands of pairs, and what comes out is a space organized by meaning. That makes zero-shot classification possible: write a caption per class, “a photo of a dog”, and pick the one closest to the image. Retrieval works the same way in either direction: embed a text query and return the closest images, or embed an image and return the closest captions.

1.2 Hierarchies and hyperbolic space

A recurring lesson in machine learning is that models built to match the structure of their data learn more from less. The space in which a model embeds its data is one such design choice, and CLIP chose the unit sphere. On a sphere every embedding has the same length, so all meaning has to live in its direction. That is enough to tell whether a caption belongs to an image, but not whether one concept is more general than another. And vision-language data arguably has that ordering: animal is more general than dog, and a caption more general than the image it describes.

Figure 3. Vision-language data read as a hierarchy, from general words down to specific images. Hover over a node to see everything it falls under.

If the data is organized this way, the obvious first step is to drop the normalization and use all of Euclidean space, letting the distance from the origin express how general something is. But trees fit poorly there. The number of nodes multiplies with every level, while the room at a given distance from the origin only grows in proportion to that distance, so deep levels get squeezed together. In hyperbolic space that room grows exponentially, as fast as a tree branches. Figure 4 shows a tree in the Poincaré disk, a map of the entire hyperbolic plane onto a disc. All edges have the same length; they only look shorter near the rim, where the map squeezes in ever more space.

Figure 4. A binary tree in the Poincaré disk, with every edge of hyperbolic length 1. Click a node to move it to the centre. The move preserves all lengths, and the tree looks the same from every node.

This is the idea behind hyperbolic vision-language models such as MERU (Desai et al., 2023), HyCoCLIP (Pal et al., 2025), ARGENT (Huynh et al., 2026) and PHyCLIP (Yoshikawa and Matsubara, 2026). They keep CLIP's recipe but embed images and text in hyperbolic space, with general concepts near the origin and specific ones further out. The encoders themselves are still ordinary Euclidean networks, though. Their outputs only reach hyperbolic space at the very end, through a single exponential map, shown in Figure 5.

Figure 5. The exponential map, the one hyperbolic step in the model. An encoder output $\mathbf{v}$ (blue) lies in the flat plane that touches the hyperboloid at its origin. The map walks the same distance in the same direction along the surface, landing at $\mathbf{x}$ (orange). Shorten $\mathbf{v}$ and see how little the map then changes: near the origin the surface is almost flat.

2 Motivation

Thanks to strong zero-shot classification and retrieval results, hyperbolic VLMs have taken off quickly. Their embedding space is also easy to read, with general concepts near the origin and specific ones further out, and new papers keep refining the losses and pushing the benchmarks. But being hyperbolic is a matter of degree. Curvature sets a length scale, and how much of the geometry a model actually uses depends on where its embeddings sit relative to that scale. Both are learned, so nothing forces a model to go far enough out for the curvature to matter. If we want to explain why these models work, we need to know how hyperbolic they really are, a question that has so far received little attention. That is what our new paper does, by looking at the exponential map: the one place where curvature enters an otherwise Euclidean network.

3 How to quantify hyperbolicity

So how do you measure how hyperbolic a model is? The curvature alone does not tell you much. What matters is how curved the space is where the embeddings actually end up. And since the curvature only enters through the exponential map, the most direct test is to compare what the model computes just before that map with what it computes just after it.

Hyperbolic VLMs build their losses out of three geometric operations: the distance between two embeddings, and the exterior angle and half-aperture of the entailment cones that encode the hierarchy. Figure 6 shows each one before and after the map; the ratio between the two tells you how much the map changed it.

Figure 6. Distance (top), exterior angle (middle) and half-aperture (bottom), before (left) and after (right) the exponential map.

Comparing each operation before and after the map gives three distortion ratios, and we derive a closed-form expression for each. They turn out to depend on just four numbers: the norms $r_1$ and $r_2$ of the encoder outputs, the angle $\theta$ between them, and the curvature $c$. The distance ratio, for example, is the hyperbolic law of cosines divided by the Euclidean one:

\[ r_d(r_1, r_2, \theta; c) = \frac{\operatorname{arcosh}\!\big( \cosh(\sqrt{c}\, r_1) \cosh(\sqrt{c}\, r_2) - \sinh(\sqrt{c}\, r_1) \sinh(\sqrt{c}\, r_2) \cos\theta \big)} {\sqrt{c}\, \sqrt{r_1^2 + r_2^2 - 2 r_1 r_2 \cos\theta}}. \]

At a given curvature, the distance ratio is therefore a function of three numbers, and fills a cube over $(r_1, r_2, \theta)$; the same goes for the exterior angle ratio. To read the cube, we fix $r_1$ and take a two-dimensional slice over $r_2$ and $\theta$, as in Figure 7. In the next section, $r_1$ will be the most common radius of a model's text embeddings.

Figure 7. The distance ratio $r_d$ fills a cube over the radii $r_1, r_2$ and the angle $\theta$ (left). Cutting it at a fixed $r_1$ gives a two-dimensional slice (right).

4 How hyperbolic are they?

4.1 Distance distortion

We trained MERU, HyCoCLIP, ARGENT and PHyCLIP on CC3M, about three million image-caption pairs. All four start at curvature $c = 1$ and push it down to its lower bound of $c = 0.1$ within the first tenth of training, while their embeddings settle at radii between roughly 0.2 and 1.1. Figure 8 cuts the cube of Figure 7 at each model's most common text radius and final curvature. In every panel, the exponential map changes distances by less than 1%.

Figure 8. The distance ratio between a text embedding at the model's most common text radius $r_1$ and an image embedding at radius $r_2$, at the converged curvature $c = 0.1$ (ViT-S/16; for PHyCLIP the mean over its 64 factors). All four panels share one colour scale.

4.2 Removing the exponential map at inference

If the exponential map is close to the identity, removing it at read-out should go unnoticed. To test this, we evaluate every trained model twice: once as usual, and once with the exponential map deleted, so that images and captions are compared by their Euclidean distance before the map. Table 1 shows that classification and retrieval scores barely move, and do not even move in a consistent direction. The publicly released MERU and HyCoCLIP checkpoints, trained on far more data, behave the same way.

4.3 Training without the exponential map

That the map can be removed after training does not mean the curvature played no role during training. To test this, we train a Euclidean control for every model from scratch: the same losses, data and schedule, but with every operation computed in Euclidean space and the exponential map replaced by the identity. The two runs only differ in the first 10,000 or so steps, while the curvature is still on its way down to its lower bound. From then on, their radii, losses and temperatures are nearly identical, and so are their final scores (Table 2). The Euclidean controls come out slightly ahead more often than not, but we do not read this as Euclidean models being better: the point is how close the two are.

5 Conclusion

We asked whether the curvature in current hyperbolic VLMs does the geometric work it is credited with, and found that it does not. The exponential map reduces to the identity early in training, leaving the embedding space effectively Euclidean for the rest of training and at inference. Consistent with this, deleting the map at read-out leaves the scores unchanged, and Euclidean controls trained with the same losses match the hyperbolic models closely. Hyperbolic VLMs have proven effective in practice, and we hope these results encourage researchers to take a critical look at their losses. With the right optimization, we believe they can do much better, and distortion ratios offer a way to measure it.
